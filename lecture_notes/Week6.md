## Goals
Today we will explore how a Statistics and Data Science major can connect to many different science-related careers.

By the end of class, you should be able to:
* name several career directions where data science is useful,
* describe the difference between a field, a role, and a sector,
* identify skills that show up across many career paths,
* and choose one realistic next step to investigate further.

This is not about choosing your whole future today.
It is about learning how to explore.

---

## Career Exploration Is a Data Problem
A career path is not one fixed answer.

It is more like a dataset you slowly build:
* What fields interest you?
* What kinds of problems do people work on?
* What skills are repeatedly requested?
* What daily work does the job actually involve?
* What courses, projects, internships, or research experiences help you prepare?

The goal is not to find the perfect job title.
The goal is to collect enough information to make better next decisions.

---

## Field, Role, and Sector
When people talk about careers, they often mix together three different ideas.

**Field:** the subject area or problem domain.
Examples: health, biotech, finance, climate, education, technology, public policy.

**Role:** what you actually do.
Examples: data analyst, statistician, biostatistician, machine learning engineer, data engineer, epidemiologist, risk analyst.

**Sector:** the type of organization.
Examples: university, hospital, startup, large company, government agency, nonprofit, consulting firm.

One major can lead to many combinations.

---

## Path 1: Biostatistics and Health Data Science
Biostatistics uses statistics to answer questions in medicine, biology, and public health.

People in this area may work on:
* clinical trials,
* electronic health records,
* disease risk prediction,
* medical imaging,
* genetics and genomics,
* and evaluating treatments or health programs.

This path is a strong fit if you like science, medicine, uncertainty, and careful evidence.

Useful skills:
* probability and statistical inference,
* regression and study design,
* R or Python,
* communication with doctors and scientists,
* and ethical thinking about health data.

---

## Path 2: Biotech and Pharmaceutical Companies
Biotech and pharmaceutical companies use data to understand biology and develop new products or treatments.

Data science can appear in:
* drug discovery,
* genomics,
* protein or molecular data,
* clinical trial operations,
* quality control,
* and product or business analytics.

This path can combine biology, computation, statistics, and industry teamwork.

Useful skills:
* programming,
* statistics and machine learning,
* basic biology or chemistry literacy,
* reproducible analysis,
* and the ability to explain results to non-data collaborators.

---

## Path 3: Technology and Applied AI
Many data science students are interested in technology companies or AI-focused work.

Common roles include:
* data analyst,
* data scientist,
* machine learning engineer,
* data engineer,
* product analyst,
* and AI evaluation or safety analyst.

The work can involve prediction, experimentation, recommendation systems, user behavior, databases, and model evaluation.

Useful skills:
* Python,
* SQL,
* machine learning,
* software habits,
* experiment design,
* and teamwork with engineers, designers, and product managers.

---

## Path 4: Finance, Risk, and Business Analytics
Finance and business careers also use statistics heavily.

Data science can support:
* risk modeling,
* fraud detection,
* forecasting,
* investment research,
* pricing,
* customer analytics,
* and decision-making under uncertainty.

This path may appeal to students who like quantitative reasoning, fast feedback, and business problems.

Useful skills:
* probability,
* regression and time series,
* SQL,
* Python,
* communication through dashboards and reports,
* and good judgment about model limits.

---

## What Do These Paths Have in Common?
Across many career paths, the same habits keep showing up.

Strong data science students learn how to:
* ask clear questions,
* clean and organize data,
* choose appropriate methods,
* evaluate uncertainty,
* notice bias and limitations,
* communicate results clearly,
* and work responsibly with other people.

Your major is not only a list of technical tools.
It is training in how to think with evidence.

---

## Freshman-Year Career Moves
You do not need an internship, publication, or perfect resume right now.

Good early moves include:
* doing well in foundational math, statistics, and programming courses,
* building one small project you can explain clearly,
* learning basic Python, R, SQL, or Git over time,
* attending a seminar, club meeting, career event, or research talk,
* asking older students what they wish they knew earlier,
* visiting office hours before you urgently need help,
* and keeping a simple list of careers or topics you want to investigate.

Small steps compound.

---


## Questions to Ask Professionals
When you meet a professor, graduate student, alumnus, or working professional, good questions are specific.

Examples:
* What does a normal week look like in your role?
* What skills do you use most often?
* What did you learn on the job that school did not teach directly?
* What kinds of projects do entry-level people work on?
* What makes someone successful in this field?
* What should a freshman do now to prepare?

Avoid asking only, "What job should I get?"
Better questions produce better information.
